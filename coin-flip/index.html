Flip history

    How to use it

    Agree the stakes first. “Heads, you drive; tails, I drive” — said before the flip, not after.
    Flip. One tap, one result — or ten at once with “Flip 10 times” for a quick series.
    Accept it. The history below records every flip in order, so the score is checkable, not arguable.

    Heads or tails: the fastest decision in the world

    Some decisions do not deserve a meeting. Who drives home, who does the dishes, which film to watch, pizza or burgers, who goes first in the game — the coin exists for exactly these: two outcomes, one gesture, no further discussion. The ritual is part of the fairness: one person calls it in the air — "heads!" — while the coin is still spinning, and the other catches it and turns it over. Nobody chose the result, so nobody can be blamed for it, and the decision is made in the second the coin lands instead of the twenty minutes the debate would have taken.

    The call has to happen before the landing — a side called after the coin is already showing is a claim on a result already known. Say the stakes out loud first, call it while it spins, and let it land. A toss re-flipped because somebody disliked the answer was never a decision, only a delay. The coin has settled arguments this way for two thousand years, because the rules are agreed before the thumb moves.

    Best of three, best of five: when one flip is not enough

    One flip settles small things. When the stakes feel bigger — a tournament's first move, a decision both sides care about — a series spreads the choice over several flips, so a single lucky bounce does not carry the whole weight. Best of three needs two wins; best of five needs three. Fix the length before the first flip, keep the score in the history below the coin, and stop exactly when somebody reaches the winning number — not one flip later, when the loser wants "just one more".

    Be honest about what a series does and does not do. It does not change the fairness: with a fair coin, either side still wins the series about half the time, because every flip is still fifty-fifty. What it changes is the feeling — a loss spread over three flips stings less than a loss decided by one. That is a real benefit, as long as nobody mistakes it for better odds.

    The streak trap: why five heads in a row changes nothing

    Watch the history fill up and the pattern-seeking part of your brain wakes up. Five heads in a row, and tails starts to feel "due" — as if the coin owes the universe a tails to balance the books. It owes nothing. A coin has no memory: after five heads, the next flip is still a plain fifty-fifty, exactly as it was before the first flip. The feeling is so strong, and so wrong, that it has a name — the gambler's fallacy — and it has separated more people from their money than any rigged game ever did.

    The maths dissolves the feeling. Five heads in a row has a chance of 1 in 32 — before the run starts. That sounds rare, until you realise a run of five flips happens constantly: flip in sets of five and you will see a clean sweep roughly once every 32 sets. The history on this page is the antidote: watch a hundred flips accumulate and the counts drift back toward half and half, while every individual flip stays independent of the one before it.

    What makes a coin flip fair — the physics of a tumbling coin

    A real coin is fair because it is symmetrical and its tumble is chaotic. The two faces weigh the same, the coin spins end over end, and the tiniest difference in the flick — a fraction more spin, a slightly different angle — decides which face is up when it lands. No amount of practice lets anyone call it reliably: the coin is a tiny chaos machine, and chaos is the fairest referee there is. A coin that lands on its edge is so rare it is a story, not a statistic — the toss effectively has two outcomes, and they are equal.

    A digital coin has to earn the same property from its random source, and not all random sources are equal. The simple random function most web pages use is fine for shuffling a playlist and wrong for anything that must be defended — its sequence can, in principle, be predicted. This flip draws from your browser's cryptographic random source, the class of randomness browsers reserve for security keys, so each result is genuinely unpredictable and equally likely. The decision is made the moment you press Flip — the spin is only the reveal, like a real coin already in the air — and the history below records every flip in order.

    Two thousand years of letting a coin decide

    The coin toss is older than most of the decisions it settles. Roman coins carried the emperor's head on one side and a ship on the other, and the game was called caput aut navia — head or ship. The device has barely changed since: two outcomes, one gesture, no argument about who chose. Cricket still opens every match with one, deciding who bats first; American football still opens every game with one, deciding who receives; and in living rooms everywhere it still settles the questions too small for a meeting and too contested for a guess — who drives, who does the dishes, which film, which restaurant.

    What makes a toss trustworthy is not the coin but the agreement around it. Both sides accept the result before the coin leaves the thumb — that is the whole contract, and it is why a toss settles things a discussion cannot. A coin that is flipped and then argued with was never a decision at all, only a delay. The flip on this page keeps the same contract: the result is drawn from your browser's cryptographic random source, the same class of randomness browsers use for security, and the history below the coin records every flip in order, so a best-of series can be checked rather than remembered.

    The coin here has no memory, and that is a feature, not a flaw. Five heads in a row does not make tails "due" — the next flip is still a coin toss, still fifty-fifty, still indifferent to everything that came before it. The history exists so you can see the run, count the series honestly, and stop when the agreed number of flips is done. Flip, accept, move on: that is the entire discipline, and it has worked for two thousand years.

    Questions, answered

    Is the coin flip really 50/50?

    Yes. Each flip is decided by your browser's crypto-grade random number generator — the same kind used for security keys — so heads and tails are equally likely on every flip, and no flip can influence the next one. Over 1,000 flips you should expect roughly 500 of each, though any short run can lean one way by chance.

    Does the animation decide the result?

    No. The result is drawn the moment you press Flip, before the coin starts turning — the spin is only the reveal, exactly like a real coin already being in the air. That is also why the result never changes if you watch the animation twice.

    Can I play best of three or best of five?

    Yes — agree the series before you start, then flip that many times and count the wins in the history below the coin. Best of three needs two wins, best of five needs three. The “Flip 10 times” button runs ten flips at once if you want a quick series, and the history records every flip in order so the score can be checked, not remembered.

    I got five heads in a row — is tails due next?

    No. A coin has no memory: after five heads, the next flip is still fifty-fifty. Five heads in a row sounds unlikely, but it happens about once in every 32 runs of five flips — the surprise is in how often runs happen, not in what comes next. Anyone selling you a system based on streaks is selling the gambler's fallacy.
