How to Convert HEIC to JPG on Windows Without Uploading

Your iPhone's photos, in a format Windows actually opens — converted on your own PC, nothing sent anywhere.

The moment Windows says no

It usually starts in File Explorer: a folder of iPhone photos transferred to a Windows PC, and half the thumbnails are blank. Double-click one and the Photos app shows an error, or nothing at all. Try to attach one to an email and the recipient writes back that it will not open. Try to upload one to a form — a job portal, a marketplace listing, a print shop — and the form answers "unsupported file type" without explaining what it actually wants. The photos are fine. The format is the problem: since the iPhone 7 era, iPhones save photos as HEIC by default, a modern format that stores the same picture in roughly half the space of a JPG — and that Windows, web forms and most of the non-Apple world still cannot open.

The fix is a conversion to JPG, the format everything accepts. The question is where that conversion happens — on somebody else's server, after you upload your camera roll to it, or on your own PC, where the photos never leave the room. This guide is the second road, step by step.

The method: convert in your browser, on your own PC

Open the HEIC to JPG converter in Edge or Chrome on your Windows PC. Then:

  1. Select the photos. Tap the box and choose the HEIC files from your Pictures folder — you can select several at once, so a whole afternoon's shoot goes in together.
  2. Pick a quality. High (92%) is the default and the right choice for nearly every photo: close to the original, far smaller than the HEIC. Best quality (95%) only earns its extra bytes for a photo you plan to print large.
  3. Convert and download. Press Convert & download and the JPGs land in your Downloads folder, one file per photo, named after the original.
  4. Check one before you use them all. Open a converted JPG in the Photos app: if it opens and looks like the photograph, the batch is good. Then attach, upload or print the JPGs wherever the HEICs were refused.

Nothing in those steps uploads a file. The converter runs in your browser tab: the file is read by your own PC, decoded and re-encoded there, and handed back as a download. Close the tab and nothing of yours remains anywhere — there is no account, no queue and no server copy to delete, because no server ever received the photos.

Converting in batches, and keeping the originals

Nobody converts one photo; the camera roll holds hundreds. Work through them in batches of twenty or thirty at a time, in the order you need them, and keep the original HEIC files as your archive — storage is cheap, reshooting is impossible. The converted JPG is the shareable copy: the one that goes into emails, forms and print shops. The HEIC stays beside it as the master, holding the full depth data and the Live Photo motion that a JPG does not carry.

Know what a conversion carries and what it leaves behind. The full resolution and the picture itself come across faithfully; for ordinary photographs in ordinary light, the converted JPG simply looks like the photo. What typically does not travel is the metadata wrapped around it — camera settings, location, depth maps and the Live Photo's motion are HEIC-container features that a JPG does not inherit — and Apple's HDR colour can render slightly flatter in a plain JPG on a non-HDR screen. For the one irreplaceable shot, keep the HEIC beside whatever you convert.

Why "without uploading" is the whole point

A camera roll is the most personal dataset most people own — children, homes, documents photographed for safekeeping, medical papers. Server-based converters ask you to hand that dataset to a stranger's machine and trust a deletion promise you cannot verify. Some keep the files for "processing", some cap free conversions and watermark the rest, and the privacy policy is the only thing standing between your photos and whatever the service does next. In-browser conversion has none of that exposure: there is nothing to delete because nothing was sent, and no promise to trust because no promise was needed.

That is also why this method is faster on a slow connection. An upload converter first has to send every megabyte of your photos up the wire — a hundred HEICs on a home connection can take longer to upload than to convert — while the in-browser converter starts the moment you pick the files. The work happens on the machine already holding the photos, which is where it belonged all along.

The codec alternative — and why it only half-works

Windows can be taught HEIC directly: Microsoft's HEIF Image Extensions, available from the Microsoft Store (free on most systems, with the video-extension sibling occasionally carrying a small charge), let Explorer and the Photos app open HEIC files as if they were JPGs. That solves viewing on your own machine, and for a household that only ever looks at its photos on one PC, it is a fair answer.

It does nothing for the four places this guide exists for: uploads, forms, other people's computers and the web. A codec on your PC does not make a portal accept the file, does not make a recipient's machine open the attachment, and does not make a print shop's system read it. That is why conversion, not codecs, remains the working answer for any photo that has to travel — and why the codec, if you install it, is a viewing convenience on top of conversion, never a replacement for it.

After conversion: what to do with the JPGs

The converted JPG opens everywhere a photo is expected to open. Attach it to an email and it arrives readable. Upload it to a job portal, a marketplace or a government form and the "unsupported file type" error is gone. Send it to a print shop and the printer reads it. Post it and the platform accepts it. If the destination also caps the file size — many forms ask for 100KB — run the converted JPG through the Compress JPG to 100KB tool, which is preset to exactly that size.

Two housekeeping habits make the converted set useful instead of chaotic. First, keep the HEIC originals in their own folder and the JPGs in another, so a month from now you can tell which is the master and which is the sending copy. Second, if you convert the same photo for a second destination, convert from the HEIC again rather than re-saving the JPG — every JPG save throws away a little more detail, while the original converts cleanly every time.

Troubleshooting: when a file still won't open

A ".jpg" that refuses to open. Some transfers rename a HEIC to .jpg without converting it — the file wears a JPG's name and a HEIC's contents. Run it through the converter anyway: a genuine JPG passes through unchanged in substance, and a disguised HEIC comes out honest.

A Live Photo that lost its motion. A Live Photo is a still plus a short video clip bound together, and converting the still leaves the motion behind — the JPG is the key moment, frozen, which is usually what was wanted. If the value was the movement, export the clip as video from the Photos app instead of converting the picture.

The converter reports an error on one file. A single corrupt or half-transferred file can stop a batch. Convert the files in smaller groups to find the broken one, re-copy it from the phone, and convert it alone — the rest of the batch is unaffected.

Windows is very old, or the browser is. The converter needs a current Edge or Chrome; a Windows 10 machine that has not updated in years may also be missing the pieces the conversion relies on. Update the browser first — that alone fixes most failures — and if the Photos app still cannot show HEICs, the codec route above is the viewing fix while conversion stays the sharing fix.
