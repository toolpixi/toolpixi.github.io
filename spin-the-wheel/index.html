Spin the Wheel

Add your own names or choices, spin the wheel, and let it decide — raffles, giveaways, classrooms, teams and yes-or-no calls.

Private by design: this tool runs entirely in your browser. Your files are never uploaded to a server, and nothing is stored.

    How to use it

    Add your choices. Type one per line — names, teams, restaurants, anything — or tap a preset for Names, Yes or No, Numbers 1–10 or Heads or Tails.
    Spin. The wheel turns for about four seconds and the pointer stops on the winner. Tick “Remove winner” first if each name should only win once.
    Use the result. The winner shows above the wheel and every spin is recorded in the history below, newest first.

    Raffles and giveaways: the wheel as a public draw

    A raffle drawn from a hat is fair, but only the hand holding the hat knows it. A wheel drawn in front of a room is fair in a way everyone can see: every entrant's name sits on a slice, the spin is public, and the pointer stops where it stops. That is why streamers spin for giveaway winners live on camera, why classrooms spin for the next reader, and why a team that cannot agree on lunch lets a wheel pick the restaurant. The decision is the same as drawing a name from a hat; the difference is that the room watched it being made, so nobody argues with it afterwards.

    For a real raffle, set the rule before the first spin: one prize per spin, winners removed so nobody wins twice, and the history as the record. Spin once per prize, in front of your audience, and screenshot the history when you are done — it lists every winner in order and settles the "did I win?" messages before they start. The names you paste never leave your browser, which matters when the list is a customer list or a class register: there is no upload, no account and nothing to delete afterwards.

    Classrooms: cold-calls without the accusation

    Every teacher knows the look: a hand goes up to answer, and three other students are certain the teacher picked a favourite. A wheel ends that argument before it starts. Paste the class list, spin, and the next reader, presenter or group leader is chosen where everyone can see it — the same chance for every name, every time, with no pattern to spot and no favourite to suspect. It works for the small jobs too: who reads the next paragraph, who goes first in the debate, which group presents first, who gets the window seat on the trip.

    The "remove winner" option earns its keep here. With it on, a full round of cold-calls runs through the whole class with nobody called twice — every student knows their turn is coming and nobody can hide at the back. With it off, each spin is independent, which is the right model for a quick question where the same student may fairly be picked again. Say which mode you are in before you spin: a class that agreed on "everyone once" and then sees a repeat will quite rightly call the draw rigged.

    Yes or no — and the decisions a wheel is actually good for

    The wheel's simplest job is the two-slice decision: yes or no, heads or tails, pizza or burgers, stay in or go out. Tap the preset, spin, and the answer arrives with a little ceremony — which is the point. A coin toss settles a decision; a wheel settles it in a way that feels like an event, and for a group decision that feeling is half the value. Nobody re-litigates a wheel's answer the way they re-litigate a coin they did not see land.

    Good wheel jobsPicking a winner, choosing who goes first, settling between options, ordering a list fairly, breaking a tie in public.
    Bad wheel jobsDecisions with real stakes you would not accept either way, anything involving money you cannot afford to lose, and choices that need reasoning rather than luck — a wheel cannot tell you which job to take.

    The rule of thumb: if you would be happy with any slice the pointer lands on, spin. If one outcome would make you spin again until you get the other one, do not spin at all — you have already decided, and the wheel is just a delay.

    Making a fair wheel: the rules before the spin

    A wheel is only as fair as the rules agreed before it turns. Three of them cover nearly every dispute. First, the list is complete: every entrant, every student, every option is on the wheel before the spin — adding a name after the result is not a correction, it is a new draw. Second, the removal rule is stated: does the winner stay in for the next round, or leave? Third, the number of spins is fixed: one spin decides, or three spins and the best of them — decided in advance, not after an unwanted result.

    Two practical details. Keep choices short — under 40 characters — so every label fits on its slice and nobody's name is cut off at the moment it wins. And cap the wheel at 24 choices: beyond that the slices get too thin to read, and a wheel nobody can read is a wheel nobody trusts. If your list is longer, split it into heats — spin once per heat, then spin the heat winners against each other.

    Wheel vs dice vs random number: which picker for which job

    ToolPixi has three pickers, and they are not interchangeable. The wheel is for choosing between named things — people, places, options — where seeing every choice on the wheel matters. The Random Number Generator is for numbers themselves: a range, a quantity, repeats or no repeats, sorted or not. And when the job is picking names rather than spinning for them, the Random Name Generator draws straight from your list without the ceremony.

    Choose by what the room needs to see. If the audience must watch the decision happen, spin the wheel. If the decision is a number — a winning ticket, a seat number, a lottery-style pick — generate the number. If it is a game roll, roll the dice. The fairness is the same in all three: the pick is made by your browser's random source, on your device, with nobody in the middle.

    A wheel is a decision you can watch happen

    A list of names in a hat is fair, but nobody can see the fairness happen — a hand goes in, a name comes out, and the room has to trust the hand. A wheel fixes the trust problem by making the draw visible: every choice sits on the wheel in plain sight, the spin is public, and the pointer stops where it stops. That visibility is why teachers spin for the next reader in class, why streamers spin for giveaway winners live on camera, and why a team that cannot agree on lunch lets a wheel choose the restaurant. The decision is the same as drawing from a hat; the difference is that everyone watched it being made, so nobody argues with it afterwards.

    The same visibility makes the wheel the honest tool for the jobs where a hidden draw would look rigged: raffle winners announced in front of a crowd, the order of presenters in a meeting, who goes first in a game, which team a new joiner lands on. A wheel that removes the winner after each spin turns one draw into a fair sequence — a full raffle order, a presentation rota, a draft order — with no name drawn twice and no quiet re-draws. And because the spin on this page is generated on your device with the browser's own random source, there is no server in the middle to favour a name, no account to sign into, and no list of your entrants sent anywhere: the names you type stay in your tab and vanish when you close it.

    One practical note before a draw that matters: say the rule out loud before you spin, not after. "The wheel picks the winner, and the winner is removed" is a rule; "spin again, that one was a joke" is a rigged draw wearing a wheel's clothes. Agree whether the winner stays in for the next round, agree how many spins decide the thing, and then let the wheel do what wheels do — decide, in public, without taking sides.

    Questions, answered

    Is the spin truly random?

    Yes. The winner is chosen with your browser's crypto-grade random number generator — the same kind used for security keys — before the wheel even starts turning. Every choice on the wheel has an equal chance, and the spin animation is just the reveal: the result is decided fairly, not by where the wheel happens to slow down.

    How many choices can I put on the wheel?

    From 2 up to 24. Fewer than 2 is not a decision, and beyond 24 the slices get too thin to read. Keep each choice under 40 characters so its label fits on its slice.

    Can I remove the winner after each spin?

    Yes — tick “Remove winner after each spin” and the winning name leaves the wheel, so the next spin picks from everyone left. That turns one wheel into a fair sequence: a raffle order, a presentation rota or a draft order with no name drawn twice. The wheel needs at least 2 choices left to spin again.

    Can I use the wheel for a yes or no decision?

    Yes — tap the “Yes or No” preset and spin. It is a fair coin toss with a ceremony attached. Just remember a wheel settles a decision you have already agreed to accept; if you would ignore a “no”, do not spin for it.
