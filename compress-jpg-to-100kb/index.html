Why 100KB is the JPG size the internet asks for

Ask for a photo anywhere online and the answer keeps coming back as two things: a JPG, and about 100KB. Social sites want a profile photo that loads instantly on a weak connection. Email wants attachments that do not stall the send. Job portals, dating sites, marketplaces and comment systems all cap uploads near 100KB for the same reason — millions of users, each with a photo, and storage that has to stay sane. JPG is the one format every one of those destinations accepts without a second thought, and 100KB is the cap they have quietly agreed on.

That agreement is what makes this page a single-purpose tool rather than a generic compressor: the answer is already known, so the page is set to it. Pick the photo, press Compress, and the file that comes back is the size the destination asked for, in the format it accepts. No guessing which quality slider lands near 100KB, no re-saving three times to creep under the limit — the tool measures the real file size at every step and stops at the best quality that fits.

What happens to your JPG at 100KB

The tool re-saves your photo as a JPEG, testing quality levels from high to low and measuring the real file size each time. At 100KB a head-and-shoulders portrait keeps nearly all of its visible detail, because the format has twice the room it gets at 50KB — faces stay distinct, background texture reads as texture, and a holiday snap looks like the holiday, not a watercolour of it. Only when the smallest sensible quality is still too big does the tool gently reduce the dimensions as well, and the preview shows you the result before anything is downloaded.

One thing to know before you rely on the result: the compressed copy is a sending copy — keep the untouched original somewhere safe, because the 100KB version is made for one destination and should not be re-compressed for the next one. And the compression happens in your browser, so the photo is never uploaded to make it smaller — the picture of your face, your family or your product stays on your device until you choose where to send it.

JPG vs PNG: which one should you be sending?

A photographJPG — photos compress gracefully, and the detail JPG throws away is detail the eye barely registers.
A screenshot or a picture of textPNG — JPG softens hard edges, so words and lines go fuzzy where PNG keeps them crisp.
A logo or graphicPNG — flat colours and sharp edges are PNG's home ground, and it keeps transparency too.
Smallest file for a photoJPG — a PNG of a photograph is often 5–10× heavier for the same pixels.
Editing again laterKeep the original — every JPG save throws away a little more, so edit from the untouched file, not the sending copy.

The commonest mistake is sending a PNG photograph: a phone screenshot saved as PNG, or a photo exported as PNG by default, arrives several megabytes heavy when the JPG of the same picture would be a few hundred kilobytes. Drop that PNG on this page and the result comes out as a 100KB JPG — the conversion and the compression in one step. If the picture is a screenshot with text you need to stay sharp, use the PNG to JPG converter at high quality instead, or keep it PNG and shrink its dimensions with the Image Resizer.

When a JPG will not fit under 100KB

Most JPGs fit 100KB comfortably, but a few resist: a very detailed landscape, a group photo with a dozen faces, or a phone photo at full 4000-pixel width. Detail is expensive — every leaf, face and letter costs bytes — and below a certain point the quality dial cannot go lower without the picture falling apart. When that happens, shrink the picture itself first: crop tighter around the subject with the Image Cropper, or reduce the dimensions with the Image Resizer, then compress the smaller image to 100KB. A 1200-pixel-wide photo at 100KB looks far better than a 4000-pixel one squeezed to the same size, because the same 100KB is spread over fewer pixels.

PNG photos deserve the same first step in reverse: convert them to JPG first with the PNG to JPG converter, since a PNG of a photograph is often several megabytes and the conversion alone usually drops it 5–10×. And if the destination genuinely allows a larger file — many do, at 200KB — take the larger size: there is no prize for fitting a smaller cap than the destination demands.

Email, social and forms: where 100KB JPGs go

Email. A 100KB JPG attaches instantly and sends fast even on a weak connection, and five of them together are half a megabyte — the same five straight from a phone camera can be 25 MB and stall the send. Attach the compressed copies and keep the originals for yourself.

Social and profile photos. Every platform recompresses what you upload to its own sizes, so the file you send only needs to arrive clean — a crisp 100KB JPG at around 1000–1200 pixels wide does that, and anything heavier is wasted bytes the platform throws away anyway.

Forms and portals. Job sites, marketplaces and application forms that cap uploads at 100KB refuse anything heavier without explaining why. This page is set to that cap because it is the one these destinations meet most often — and if a particular form states a different number, the Custom box takes it exactly.

100KB in JPG: the size every upload box understands

JPG is the one format every upload box, inbox and social site accepts without a second thought, and 100KB is the size those boxes keep asking for. The pair works because JPG was built for exactly this trade: photographs compress gracefully, losing detail the eye barely registers, while the file shrinks to a fraction of its original weight. A 100KB JPG is light enough to attach five at a time to an email, small enough for any profile-photo slot, and sharp enough that the person looking at it sees the photograph, not the compression.

The habit that makes this page useful is the same one the other size pages teach: the 100KB file is a sending copy. Keep the untouched original — the full-resolution JPG from the camera — and make a fresh 100KB copy for each place that asks for one. Re-compressing a 100KB copy for the next form throws away detail twice, while the original compresses cleanly every time. One original, as many sending copies as there are forms — each made fresh, each at exactly the size its destination demands.

Questions, answered

How do I compress a JPG to exactly 100KB?

Choose your photo above — 100KB is already selected — then press Compress. The tool lowers quality step by step until the file fits 100KB at the best quality that fits, and shows you a preview before you download. The result downloads as a JPG.

My photo is a PNG — can I still use this page?

Yes. Drop the PNG in and the result comes out as a JPG at 100KB — photographs usually shrink 5–10× in the step, because JPG stores photos far more efficiently than PNG. The exception is screenshots and graphics with sharp text: JPG softens hard edges, so if the picture is mostly words, keep it as PNG and shrink its dimensions with the Image Resizer instead.

Is 100KB enough for a profile photo or a social post?

Yes — and usually more than enough. Social platforms recompress every upload to their own sizes anyway, so a crisp 100KB JPG at around 1000–1200 pixels wide arrives looking like the original. A profile-photo slot displays at only a few hundred pixels, so 100KB is generous headroom, not a squeeze.

Can I attach a 100KB JPG to an email?

Yes — a 100KB JPG attaches instantly, sends fast on slow connections and stays far under every mail service's attachment limit. Five photos at 100KB each total half a megabyte; the same five straight from a phone camera can be 25 MB and stall or bounce on a weak connection.
