This calculator models a standard fixed-rate mortgage with monthly payments. Tax and insurance figures are estimates — real escrow amounts vary by county, insurer and lender, and PMI rules differ by loan type. Use the result to compare homes and offers, not as a quote; a lender's Loan Estimate is the binding document.

How to use it

Type the home price and your down payment as a percentage.
Add the rate and term from the offer you are weighing up.
Fill in tax, insurance and PMI — the three lines lender adverts leave out — then read the true monthly total and the schedule.

Why the advertised payment is never the payment

Run the standard example through the calculator above: a $400,000 home, 20% down, 6.5% for 30 years. The loan payment — the number in every advert — is $2,022.62 a month. Add property tax at 1.1% of the price ($366.67 a month) and insurance at $1,800 a year ($150 a month) and the real cheque is about $2,539 a month, a quarter more than the headline. Drop the down payment below 20% and PMI joins in as well. None of these lines is hidden exactly; they are just never in the big font.

The schedule underneath matters just as much. In the first year of that loan, barely one dollar in seven of what you pay touches the balance — just 14.7% of it — and the rest is interest. That is not a trick, it is how amortization works: interest is charged on what you still owe, and at the start you owe almost everything. It is also why the total interest figure deserves a hard look before the monthly one. The same $320,000 at the same rate over 15 years instead of 30 costs roughly $226,000 less in interest — the monthly payment rises, but the loan stops being a second purchase price paid to the bank.

Everything runs on your device. The figures you type are never sent anywhere, so you can model the house you are actually looking at — price, taxes and all — without handing your finances to a lead-generation form.

The formula behind the payment

The loan part of the payment is one formula, the same one every lender uses: M = P × i × (1 + i)n ÷ ((1 + i)n − 1), where P is the amount borrowed, i is the monthly rate (the yearly rate ÷ 12) and n is the number of payments (years × 12). For the example above — $320,000 borrowed at 6.5% over 30 years — i is 0.005417 and n is 360, and the formula returns the $2,022.62 the calculator shows. The tax, insurance and PMI lines are then simply divided by 12 and added on top; there is no hidden lender maths beyond that.

The second check is the payment's weight in a real budget: the Salary Converter restates your pay monthly so you can see the payment as a share of it, and if a car payment is competing for the same money, the Auto Loan Calculator applies the same total-cost honesty to the car.

The monthly payment is four bills wearing one coat

A mortgage quote leads with a single monthly figure, but that figure is a bundle: principal and interest on the loan itself, property tax, homeowner's insurance, and — when the down payment is under 20% — PMI, the insurance that protects the lender rather than you. This calculator unbundles them, because the parts behave differently: tax and insurance drift upward over the years, PMI falls away once your equity crosses the line, and only the loan part follows the amortization schedule, where early payments are mostly interest and late ones mostly principal.

The term is where the real money hides. Take a $350,000 loan at 6.5%: over 30 years the loan part costs $2,212.24 a month and $446,406 of interest across the life of the loan. The same loan over 15 years costs $3,048.88 a month — about $837 more — but only $198,798 of interest, saving $247,608. Nothing about the house changed; only the number of months the balance spent being rented. Extra payments pull the same lever from the other side: an extra $200 a month on that 30-year loan clears it in 285 months instead of 360 and saves about $108,000 of interest.

Questions, answered

How is a mortgage payment calculated?

The loan part uses the standard amortization formula: the amount borrowed, the monthly interest rate (yearly rate ÷ 12) and the number of monthly payments. On top of that, most lenders collect property tax and home insurance monthly in escrow, and PMI if your down payment is under 20% — this calculator adds all of them so the total is the cheque you actually write.

What is PMI and when does it apply?

Private Mortgage Insurance — a monthly premium the lender charges when the down payment is under 20% of the home price, typically 0.3%–1% of the loan per year. It protects the lender, not you, and it usually drops off once you reach 20% equity. Enter 0 if your down payment is 20% or more — the calculator switches PMI off automatically below that line.

Does the amortization schedule change if I pay extra each month?

Yes — extra payments cut the balance faster, so less interest accrues and the loan ends early. This schedule shows the standard path with no extra payments; use it as the baseline, then compare any overpayment plan against the total interest shown here.

How much house can I afford on my salary?

Lenders start from the 28/36 rule: the full housing payment — principal, interest, tax, insurance and PMI together — should stay under 28% of your gross monthly income, and housing plus every other debt payment under 36%. On a $75,000 salary that caps the housing payment at $1,750 a month. Work backwards from your cap here: lower the home price until the monthly total this calculator shows fits under it.
