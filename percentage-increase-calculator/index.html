Percentage Increase Calculator

How to use it

For a change: enter the old value and the new value — the calculator gives the percentage increase or decrease, and the absolute change.
For a price after a rise: enter the value and the percentage — it shows the price after that % increase and after that % decrease.
Read the note under the answer — it spells out the change in plain words, so you can quote it.

The formula: (new − old) ÷ old × 100

A percentage change always measures the difference against where you started. A salary that rises from $2,000 to $2,200 has grown by $200 — and $200 is 10% of $2,000, so the increase is 10%. The same $200 rise on a $400 starting point would be a 50% increase: the absolute change is identical, the percentage is not, because the starting point is different.

That starting-point rule is also why increases and decreases are not symmetric. A price that rises 50% (from 100 to 150) and then falls 50% does not return to 100 — it falls to 75, because the second 50% is measured against 150, not 100. To get back to the start after a 50% rise takes a 33.33% fall. Whenever a percentage sounds too good, check what it was measured against.

Everyday uses

Pay rises and prices: a 10% rise on $200 is $220; a 25% discount on $80 leaves $60. Investments: a portfolio that goes from $1,000 to $1,150 is up 15% — and a fall back to $1,000 from $1,150 is a 13.04% drop, not 15%. Growth: followers, revenue or traffic that doubles is a 100% increase; tripling is 200%. The same formula answers all of them — and the calculator above does it in both directions.

The new figure in words

A pay rise usually lands in an offer letter or a revised contract, where the new salary is written in words as well as figures. The number to words converter writes the new figure out in full — millions in the international system, or lakh and crore in the Indian system.

The raise, the rent rise and the reversed percentage

"Prices went up 8%." "Your salary increases by 5%." "The rent is rising from 900 to 990 — is that the 10% the landlord promised?" Percentage increase is the language of every unwelcome letter and half the welcome ones, and verifying it takes one formula: the change divided by the original, times 100. The original is the anchor — every error in this territory comes from dividing by the new number instead, which flatters whoever is presenting the rise.

The skill that separates the fluent from the fooled is running the machine backwards. If a jacket costs 84 after a 30% discount, what was the original price? Not 84 plus 30% of 84 — that overshoots, because the discount was taken from the original, not from 84. The true reversal divides by what remains: 84 ÷ 0.70 = 120. The same reversal recovers a pre-tax price from a tax-included one, and a pre-raise salary from a payslip. This calculator shows the forward answer plainly; once you can see the anchor value in the result, the backward jobs become the same shape.

And a sanity habit worth keeping: equal percentage moves do not cancel. Up 50% then down 50% leaves you at three-quarters of where you started, because the second percentage acted on a bigger base. Whenever a chart, a fund report or a landlord's letter leans on percentages, ask "percent of what, starting when?" — the two questions this page is built to answer.

Questions, answered

How do I calculate a percentage increase?

Subtract the old value from the new one, divide by the old value, then multiply by 100: (new − old) ÷ old × 100. A rise from 50 to 75 is (75 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = 50%.

What is the percentage increase from 50 to 75?

50% — the value grew by 25, and 25 is half of 50. The same maths in reverse: a fall from 75 to 50 is a 33.33% decrease, because the 25 lost is a third of the 75 you started from.

What if the old value is zero?

A percentage change from zero is undefined — you cannot divide by zero, so no percentage exists. This calculator says so plainly instead of inventing a number.

What is the difference between a percentage increase and percentage points?

If a rate rises from 20% to 25%, that is a rise of 5 percentage points — but a 25% increase, because 5 is a quarter of 20. Percentage points measure the gap; percent increase measures the gap relative to where you started.
